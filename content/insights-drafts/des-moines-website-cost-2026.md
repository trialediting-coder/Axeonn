## Why quotes vary so much

Four things drive the number on a web design quote. Knowing them makes every proposal easier to read.

### 1. Who is building it

A DIY builder like Wix or Squarespace charges very little because you are the labor. A freelancer charges for their hours and usually nothing else. An agency charges for a project manager, a designer, a developer, and an account manager, and every one of those people is in the price whether or not you talk to them.

The cost breakdown from Elementor puts freelancer builds for a basic five-to-eight-page site at roughly $2,500 to $7,000 and agency builds for the same scope at $5,000 to $15,000. The site is often the same. The overhead is not.

### 2. Template or custom

A template with your logo dropped in is cheap to produce and looks like every other template. A custom design is built around how your specific business earns money: what a [roofing company](/solutions/roofing) needs on its homepage is not what a [dental practice](/solutions/dental) needs, and neither looks like a [law firm](/solutions/law-firms).

Custom does not have to mean expensive. It means the layout, the copy hierarchy, and the calls to action were decided for your business instead of inherited from a theme.

### 3. Design only, or design plus the systems behind it

This is the line most quotes hide. A "website" can mean a set of pages, or it can mean pages plus the plumbing that turns a visitor into a booked job: instant lead alerts, a pipeline that qualifies inquiries, automated follow-up by text and email, and online scheduling.

The pages alone will not answer a lead who fills out a form at 9 p.m. The systems will. When you compare quotes, ask what happens after someone submits a form. If the answer is "it emails you," you are buying pages.

### 4. How they bill

Hourly billing means the final number is unknown until the project ends. Proposal-based billing means a multi-week sales process before you see a number. Flat-rate billing means the price is the price.

We publish flat prices because we would rather lose a project on price than win one on a quote nobody can verify.

## What a Des Moines business actually needs the site to do

Before you spend anything, be clear on the job the site has to do. For most local businesses in the metro, it is three things.

**Get found locally.** [Backlinko's compilation of local SEO statistics](https://backlinko.com/local-seo-stats) reports that 46 percent of Google searches have local intent, and that 76 percent of people who search for something "near me" visit a business within a day. If your site is not built to show up for "HVAC repair West Des Moines" or "dentist Ankeny," the design does not matter, because nobody sees it. That is what [SEO, AEO and GEO built into the site](/marketing-solutions/seo) is for, and why we include it in every build rather than selling it separately.

**Load fast on a phone.** Google's own research, [published in its AdSense documentation](https://support.google.com/adsense/answer/7450973), found that 53 percent of mobile visits are abandoned when a page takes longer than three seconds to load. Most of your visitors are on a phone, standing in a driveway or a waiting room. A slow site is an invisible site.

**Look credible next to your reviews.** [BrightLocal's 2025 Local Consumer Review Survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey-2025/) found that 71 percent of consumers read online reviews regularly when browsing local businesses. Your reviews get them to your site. The site has to confirm what the reviews promised in the first few seconds.

Everything else is nice to have. A site that does those three things and puts a phone number and a booking button where a thumb can reach them will outperform a prettier site that does not.

## Questions to ask before you sign with anyone

Take these into any conversation with any web designer in Iowa, including us.

1. **Do I own the site, the code, and the design files when it's done?** If the answer is anything other than yes, you are renting.
2. **Is the price fixed, or is it an estimate?** Ask what would make it go up.
3. **What happens when a lead submits a form?** Pages or systems.
4. **Will it show up for local searches, and how do you know?** Ask to see the structured data and the local pages.
5. **How fast does it load on a phone?** Ask for a Core Web Vitals result on a site they built, not a promise.
6. **Who do I talk to after launch?** The person who built it, or an account manager.
