# How to Choose a Marketing Agency for Your Practice in Des Moines: 9 Questions to Ask Before You Sign (2026)

If you own or manage a dental practice, a med spa, a law firm or an accounting firm in the Des Moines metro, you've probably noticed that every marketing agency says roughly the same thing. More traffic. Better rankings. A stronger brand. Then the proposal shows up and you can't tell what you're paying for, or how you'd know if it worked.

This guide is the checklist we'd want a practice owner to use. It covers what a practice should expect from a marketing agency in 2026, the nine questions that separate the useful ones from the expensive ones, and the red flags worth walking away from. We're based in West Des Moines, so our own answers are in here too. Hold us to the same list.

## Start with the outcome, not the services

Most agency conversations start with a menu: a website, SEO, ads, social media, maybe video. That's backwards. The menu is the tools. What you want is a full schedule of the right patients or clients.

So before you talk to anyone, write down three numbers:

- How many calls and web inquiries you get in a normal month, and how many come in after hours.
- How many of those turn into booked consultations or appointments.
- What one new patient or client is worth to your practice over a year.

Those numbers tell you what a fair budget looks like, and they give you a baseline. An agency that can't be measured against your baseline is asking you to judge it on feel.

## What marketing actually has to do for a practice

For most practices, marketing comes down to three things.

**Get found.** When someone in Ankeny or Urbandale searches for a dentist, a Botox appointment, a divorce attorney or a CPA, you need to show up on Google, on Google Maps and increasingly in AI answers from tools like ChatGPT. We wrote a full guide on [how to get your practice recommended by ChatGPT](/insights/how-to-get-your-business-recommended-by-chatgpt) because that last one is changing fast.

**Get chosen.** Once they find you, they compare you. [BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/) found that 97 percent of consumers read online reviews when choosing a local business. Your reviews, your providers' photos and the first few seconds on your website decide whether they call you or the next name on the list.

**Get booked.** This is the part most agencies skip. An inquiry nobody answers is worth nothing, and timing matters. The [MIT and InsideSales lead response study](https://25649.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na2.net/hub/25649/file-13535879-pdf/docs/mit_study.pdf) found that inquiries contacted within five minutes were about 21 times more likely to qualify than those contacted after thirty. For a practice, the gap is usually the hours when the front desk is closed or busy with the patient in front of them.

An agency that only works on getting found sends you clicks. An agency that works on all three helps fill the schedule. That's the first thing to check.

## The 9 questions to ask any marketing agency

Take these into every sales call, including ours.

### 1. "How will we measure whether this is working?"

The answer should be calls, web inquiries and booked consultations, counted against your baseline. If the answer is impressions, reach or "brand awareness," you're paying for activity. Ask to see a sample of the report you'd get each month.

### 2. "What happens when someone reaches out at 9 p.m.?"

This one question tells you more than the whole pitch deck. Good answers involve an instant text back when a call is missed, a reply to web inquiries within a minute or two, and online booking so people can pick a consultation time themselves. "It sends you an email" means nobody follows up until someone opens the inbox the next morning.

### 3. "Who owns the website, the ad accounts, the Google Business Profile and our contact data?"

Your domain, Google Business Profile, ad accounts and patient or client contact data should be in your name. Ad accounts should be on your card, so the history stays with you. Ask exactly when the website itself becomes yours, and which parts are a service that stops if you leave. A straight answer is a good sign. "You own everything" with no detail is not.

### 4. "What are the terms, and how do I get out if you don't deliver?"

Agencies run on everything from month-to-month to multi-year contracts. The length matters less than what protects you inside it. Ask for the term in writing, what you pay if you leave early, and whether there is an exit tied to the agency doing what it said it would. A longer agreement with a clear performance exit is safer than a short one with vague deliverables.

### 5. "Do you mark up ad spend?"

If you run Google ads, ask whether the agency takes a cut of what you spend. Some charge a percentage of spend on top of their fee, which means they earn more when you spend more, whether or not it's working. A flat management fee with spend paid directly to Google keeps the incentives honest. Also ask how they handle Local Services Ads: Google runs its own [screening and verification](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/6224841) for those, so nobody can promise you the badge.

### 6. "Can you show me how the system works, not just a logo wall?"

A logo wall tells you who paid an agency once. It doesn't tell you what happens to your inquiries. Ask to see the actual tools: the report, the inbox, what a patient receives when a call is missed. A newer agency may have fewer names to show. What matters is whether what they show you is specific and checkable.

### 7. "Who will I actually talk to?"

At large agencies you'll often meet a senior strategist on the sales call and an account manager after you sign. Ask by name who does the work and who answers when something breaks. A local team you can reach by phone is worth more than a ticket queue.

### 8. "What do you commit to, in writing?"

Nobody can honestly guarantee a #1 ranking or a number of new patients, and you should walk away from anyone who does. Google doesn't sell rankings, and nobody controls how many people need a crown or a tax return this month. But an agency can commit to what it controls: how fast the system is live, how fast inquiries get a reply, and what happens if it misses.

### 9. "What will this cost me per month, all in?"

Add it all up: the implementation fee, the monthly fee, ad spend, add-ons and anything billed hourly. Then compare it to the value of one new patient or client from your notes above. If a few new patients or clients a month would cover the whole thing, the math can work. If you'd need dozens, it doesn't.

## Red flags to walk away from

- **Guaranteed rankings or a guaranteed number of new patients.** Nobody controls either.
- **No price until a discovery call and a second proposal call.** That usually means the price depends on what they think you'll pay.
- **Long agreements with no performance exit.** Especially when the deliverables are vague.
- **Reports full of numbers you can't connect to booked appointments.** Impressions don't fill a schedule.
- **Accounts or data in the agency's name.** Your domain, ad accounts, Google profile and contact data should belong to you.
- **Intake forms that ask for health details.** For a dental practice or med spa, a web form has no reason to collect symptoms or insurance information.
- **One package for every kind of business.** A dental practice and a law firm need different pages, different searches and a different first impression.
